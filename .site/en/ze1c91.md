## Why Do We Need These Properties?

Before you get into any type of encryption (Symmetric, Asymmetric, Hashing, Digital Signatures...), you need a **fixed yardstick** to measure any Cryptographic technique against — so you can ask yourself: "What exactly does this technique protect? And what does it leave unprotected?"

These 5 principles are that yardstick.

> [!NOTE]
> If you've studied Security in general before, you've probably heard of the **CIA Triad** (Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability) as a general foundation for any security system. Here, specifically in the context of Cryptography, **Availability** isn't one of the properties that encryption provides directly (encryption has nothing to do with whether the system stays up or not). In its place, we've added three principles that matter more in the context of Cryptography: **Authentication, Non-repudiation, Key Exchange**, to cover every angle of handling data and identity securely.

---

## 1. Confidentiality

**Definition:** Ensuring that sensitive information is available only to the people authorized to view it, and preventing anyone unauthorized from intercepting or reading the data while it's in transit or in storage.

**Real-world example:** When you send a message on an app with End-to-End Encryption, even if someone intercepts the message in transit, they won't be able to read its content because it's encrypted.

> [!TIP]
> Think of it as "privacy of the content" — who is allowed to see what's inside the message.

---

## 2. Integrity

**Definition:** Ensuring that data hasn't been changed, tampered with, or corrupted during its lifecycle, providing confidence that the information has remained accurate and trustworthy from the point of origin to the point where it reaches the recipient.

**Real-world example:** If you download a file from the internet and compare its Hash with the Hash published on the official website, and they match, then the file wasn't changed during the download.

> [!TIP]
> Think of it as "soundness of the content" — is what arrived exactly the same as what was sent, with no modification at all?

---

## 3. Authentication

**Definition:** The process of verifying that a particular entity (such as a user or a system) really is who it claims to be. Among the most common ways to achieve this: passwords, Digital Certificates, and biometric verification.

**Real-world example:** When you log in to your bank account and enter your Username and Password, the bank verifies that you really are the account owner and not someone impersonating you.

> [!TIP]
> Think of it as "proof of identity" — is the other party really who they claim to be?

---

## 4. Non-repudiation

**Definition:** Preventing a party from denying their involvement in a specific operation or transaction after it has actually taken place. It provides conclusive, irreversible proof of the origin of a message and its receipt, and it's usually achieved using Digital Signatures and Secure Logging.

**Real-world example:** If you sign an electronic contract with a Digital Signature, you can't later deny that you signed it, because the signature is tied to your Private Key and yours alone.

> [!TIP]
> Think of it as "no denial" — mathematical proof that you really are the one who did it, and you can't take it back.

---

## 5. Key Exchange

**Definition:** A method that allows two parties to share Cryptographic Keys securely over an Insecure Channel. This process is essential for establishing secure communications before any sensitive data is transmitted.

**Real-world example:** When the browser and the server agree on a Session Key at the start of an HTTPS connection (as will happen in the TLS Handshake), without anyone being able to copy that Key even if they're monitoring the entire connection.

> [!TIP]
> Think of it as "securely agreeing on the secret" — how two parties arrive at a shared secret without a third party learning it, even if that party is watching everything between them.

## Quick Reference Table

| Property | Question It Answers | Technical Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Confidentiality** | Who can read the data? | Encryption |
| **Integrity** | Has the data been changed? | Hashing |
| **Authentication** | Who is this, really? | Passwords, Certificates |
| **Non-repudiation** | Can they deny having done it? | Digital Signatures |
| **Key Exchange** | How do we agree on a secret securely? | Diffie-Hellman |

## Summary

- Every Cryptography system is measured against 5 core principles: **Confidentiality, Integrity, Authentication, Non-repudiation, Key Exchange**
- **Confidentiality:** protecting the content from unauthorized reading
- **Integrity:** making sure the data hasn't been changed
- **Authentication:** verifying the identity of the other party
- **Non-repudiation:** preventing denial after the action has been completed
- **Key Exchange:** agreeing on a secret key securely even if the channel is insecure
- The general CIA Triad (which includes Availability) is different from these 5 principles, because Availability isn't a property that encryption provides directly
- Every upcoming encryption topic will be measured against these principles so you understand exactly what it achieves and what it leaves unprotected

## SOC Analyst Perspective

- These 5 principles aren't just theory — any professional Incident Report describes the impact of an incident based on them (for example: "The attack affected Confidentiality because the data was leaked, but it didn't affect Integrity because the files weren't changed")
- When you analyze any attack (such as a Data Breach or a MITM Attack), ask yourself: "Exactly which of these 5 principles was violated?" This question helps you determine the severity of the incident and the steps needed to handle it
- MITRE ATT&CK categorizes many tactics based on these principles — for example, **Exfiltration** targets Confidentiality, and **Impact/Data Manipulation** targets Integrity
- In Threat Intelligence and Compliance reports (such as ISO 27001), you'll find these principles recurring as the basis for Risk Assessment
